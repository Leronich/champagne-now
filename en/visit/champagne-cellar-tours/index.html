Champagne Cellar Tours: The Complete Guide to Visiting Champagne Houses

Most wine regions send you out into the light. Champagne sends you down a staircase into the dark, and that reversal is the whole character of a visit here. What follows is how to choose a cellar, what it costs, when to come and how to book it.

What it costs €15–230 per person, depending far more on what you taste than on how far you walk. Where to start Épernay, where a dozen houses sit within fifteen minutes of the station.
Illustration
Why Champagne Is Different

In Burgundy or the Douro, a visit means standing among vines while someone points at a hillside and explains why this row differs from the next. The landscape does the teaching. Champagne can do that too, but it is not what the region is built around. Here the essential thing happens underground, in galleries cut into chalk, at a temperature that has not meaningfully changed since the Romans opened the first pits.

Those pits are the reason. Beneath Reims lie the crayères — quarries dug in the 1st century for building stone, tapering upward to a narrow opening at the surface like inverted funnels, the deepest of them some thirty-eight metres down. The Romans wanted the chalk. Nineteen centuries later the houses wanted the holes: cool, humid, dark, structurally sound and already there. Beneath Épernay the galleries were cut later and more deliberately, but the principle is identical, and the total under that one small town runs to around 110 kilometres holding more than 200 million bottles. In 2015 UNESCO inscribed the hillsides, houses and cellars of Champagne on the World Heritage list, and the cellars were not an afterthought in that decision. They are the working architecture of a wine that could not exist without them.

What this means in practice is that a champagne visit has a shape other regions do not. You arrive at a street entrance, often through a courtyard that looks like a bank. You go down. It is between roughly 10 and 12°C at the bottom, all year, and the damp makes it feel cooler than that. You walk, sometimes for a kilometre or more, past stacked bottles and riddling racks and the occasional gyropalette doing mechanically what a cellar hand once did by wrist. Someone explains second fermentation, riddling and disgorgement — the three ideas that turn a thin, sharp base wine into champagne. Then you come back up into daylight and are handed a glass, and the wine tastes different than it would have an hour earlier, because you now know what it cost to make.

Expect ninety minutes for a standard visit, of which perhaps twenty-five are spent tasting. Expect stairs, and a good number of them, with lifts rare. Expect to book, because almost nothing here works on a walk-in basis.

Houses in Épernay

Épernay is the practical answer for a first visit. The Avenue de Champagne runs west from the town centre for about a kilometre, and the great houses are lined along it gate after gate, with the cellars threading underneath in a network that pays no attention to the property lines above. You can walk from the station to the far end in twenty minutes. Two visits in a day is comfortable, three is a full day, four is a mistake.

Houses in Reims

Reims is the other half of the answer, and a different kind of day. The houses here are not on one street but scattered across a working city, which means moving between visits and a cathedral in the middle of it all. What Reims has that Épernay does not is the crayères themselves — the Gallo-Roman chalk pits, used as cellars by Ruinart, Taittinger and Veuve Clicquot, and the specific thing UNESCO had in mind.

Grower Producers

A grower — a récoltant-manipulant, the letters RM in small type on the label — makes champagne from vines the family owns rather than from grapes bought across the region. It is a different business and a very different visit. There is no visitor centre, no scripted route and often no fixed departure time. You are received in a working cellar by someone who will be back on the tractor that afternoon, and the tasting tends to be more generous and much less choreographed than at a house whose name you already knew.

The trade-off is real. Growers are harder to schedule, more likely to cancel during harvest, and rarely equipped for large groups. But if you want to understand why one village tastes unlike its neighbour four kilometres away, this is where you find out, because the person pouring farms both slopes.

How to Choose

The price of a visit is not the price of the walk. Every cellar in Champagne is cold, chalky and impressive; what separates an €18 ticket from a €200 one is almost entirely what gets poured at the end, and secondarily how many people are standing next to you while it happens. Read the listing for the wines, not for the adjectives.

€15–25, the grower visit. One family, one cellar, one or two wines. No production values whatsoever. Frequently the most interesting hour of the trip, and almost always the best value in the region.

€25–45, the standard house tour. The main offer at Mercier, Moët & Chandon and their neighbours. Ninety minutes underground with a guide, ending in a glass of the entry cuvée. Reliable, well organised, and the right choice for a first visit.

€45–90, the tasting upgrade. The same route with better glasses at the end of it: vintage wines, a rosé alongside the brut, sometimes a prestige cuvée in a smaller pour. Worth the difference if you already know you like the house.

€90–230, private and immersive. A guide to yourselves, access to parts of the cellar the standard tour skips, older bottles, occasionally a meal or a vineyard walk attached. The upper end of this band buys scarcity above all: a room with four people in it instead of twenty-four.

Four formats recur across all of those bands. A guided tour is the default — cellar plus tasting, fixed departures, around ninety minutes. A tasting only skips the walk and puts three or four wines in front of you in a room above ground, which is the efficient choice if you are visiting your third cellar of the trip and have seen enough chalk. A private tour is the same content at your own pace with your own guide. An immersive experience is the catch-all for anything longer: blending workshops, sabrage lessons, a morning on the harvest, lunch in the vines. These sell out earliest and cancel most readily, so book them first and build the rest of the day around them.

Practical Information

Getting there from Paris. Épernay is a direct regional train from Gare de l'Est, a little over an hour and a quarter, with departures roughly every two hours and fares in the €25–35 range. Reims is faster, around forty-five minutes by TGV from the same station, and is the better arrival point if you are staying the night. Both towns are walkable on arrival and neither requires a car. A car only becomes useful for growers out in the villages, and it immediately raises the question of who is not tasting.

When to go. May, June and early October are the comfortable months. September is harvest: the region is at its most alive and its least available, because many producers stop receiving visitors entirely for three to four weeks. If September is your only option, book far ahead and confirm directly with the house. Winter is quiet, inexpensive and perfectly pleasant underground, though the vineyards give you nothing to look at and some small estates close.

How to book. Reserve online in advance and treat English-language slots as the scarce resource — they are usually one or two departures a day, not all of them. A week ahead is enough in the shoulder seasons; a month for September or for anything private. Confirm the meeting point, which at several houses is a courtyard entrance rather than the address printed on the avenue.

On the day. Bring a jacket regardless of the weather above ground, and wear shoes you can grip damp chalk in. Most tours involve a substantial staircase and no lift; if that is a problem, say so when booking, because a few houses do have step-free routes but they are not offered unless asked for. Houses commonly close between noon and 2pm, which shapes a two-visit day more than the tours themselves do.

Questions

Do you need to book a champagne cellar tour in advance?

Almost always, yes. The large houses on the Avenue de Champagne run fixed departures with limited places, and English-language slots are the first to fill. Growers are stricter still: a small estate has no visitor department, so the person showing you the cellar is the person who made the wine, and they need to know you are coming. A week ahead is comfortable in spring and autumn. For September, when harvest closes many doors entirely, book a month out and confirm that visits are running at all.

How much does a champagne house tour cost?

Roughly €15 to €230 per person, and the spread is mostly about what is in the glass rather than how long you walk. Around €15 to €25 buys a grower's tour with one or two wines. €25 to €45 is the standard large-house visit with a glass of the entry cuvée. €50 to €90 brings vintage or prestige wines into the tasting. Above €100 you are paying for private access, a smaller group, an older bottle, or all three.

When is the best time of year to visit Champagne cellars?

May, June and early October are the easiest months: the vineyards look like something, the region is not full, and the houses are running their normal schedules. September is the most atmospheric and the most awkward, because harvest is under way — which is exactly what you came to see and exactly why many producers suspend visits. Winter is quiet and inexpensive, and the cellars are the same temperature they always are, but the vines are bare and some smaller estates close entirely.

Can you visit champagne houses without speaking French?

At the large houses, yes, without any difficulty — Moët & Chandon, Mercier and their neighbours run scheduled English tours daily. At grower estates it varies by family. Many speak good English and enjoy the practice; some do not, and will say so when you book. If your French is limited, ask when reserving rather than on the doorstep.

How cold are champagne cellars and what should you wear?

The chalk holds the cellars between roughly 10 and 12°C all year, and the humidity makes that feel colder than the number suggests. Bring a jacket even in August. Wear shoes with grip: the galleries are damp chalk and worn stone, and most tours involve a long staircase with no lift alternative.

Is Épernay or Reims better for cellar visits?

Épernay if you want cellars, Reims if you want a city. The Avenue de Champagne puts a dozen houses within a fifteen-minute walk of each other and of the station, which makes two or three visits in a day genuinely easy. Reims has the cathedral, better restaurants, more hotels and the Gallo-Roman chalk pits, but its houses are spread across the city and part of the day goes on taxis.
